What batteries can teach us about lab-grown meat

For decades, grid batteries were a pipe dream. Then, almost overnight, they weren't. Lab-grown, or "cultivated," meat is in the pipe-dream phase now. What would it take for it to follow the same curve?

The short version

Batteries spent two decades as a "never competitive" technology, then became cheaper than new coal and gas in about five years once a handful of forces lined up.

Cultivated meat is where batteries were in the laptop era: costs are falling fast, but from far above parity, with no patient sponsor yet and real political resistance.

The battery playbook suggests ingredients and hybrid products could break through within a decade. Steaks will take longer. "Never" is the least likely outcome.

For most of the past twenty years, batteries were the punchline of energy debates. Too expensive, too small, dependent on subsidies. The standard line was that clean power would never compete with coal and gas on cost, and that storing it would be even further out of reach.

As recently as 2020, storing and delivering a megawatt-hour of electricity from a four-hour battery cost around $185, roughly double new coal or gas. Then the line in the chart above fell off a cliff. By 2025 the cost had dropped to $78, and BloombergNEF estimates it will reach about $72 in 2026. Battery storage is now cheaper than new coal and new gas plants, while gas plants got 16% more expensive in 2025 alone.

In plain terms: levelized cost

Levelized cost of electricity is the total cost of building and running a power source over its lifetime, divided by the electricity it delivers. It lets you compare a battery, a gas plant and a solar farm on the same yardstick: dollars per megawatt-hour.

4 years

The time it took annual battery installations worldwide to grow from 10 gigawatts to more than 100. In 2025 the world added 112 GW of storage, up 48% in a year.

The collapse looked sudden, but it was twenty years in the making. Lithium-ion costs had been falling steadily for two decades. What changed in the past few years is that several forces lined up at once: a cheaper battery chemistry, a flood of Chinese factory capacity, cheap solar power that needed somewhere to go, and new demand from data centers. When they aligned, batteries crossed price parity, and adoption began to look less like a choice and more like an inevitability.

Is lab-grown meat the next pipe dream?

Cultivated meat hears the same caveats batteries heard for years: nobody will want it, it will never be cheap enough, and the economics don't work at scale.

Cultivated meat is real animal meat grown from cells in steel tanks rather than raised on a farm. The first cultivated burger, unveiled in 2013, cost about $330,000. Today small amounts are sold in a handful of countries, mostly in restaurants and at luxury prices. Funding has collapsed, several leading companies have closed, and seven US states have banned it.

In plain terms: how it's made

Take a small sample of cells from an animal. Put them in a steel tank of warm nutrient broth, much like brewing beer, where they multiply for weeks. Harvest them, then form them into food, usually blended with plant or fungal ingredients for texture.

On the surface, cultivated meat looks nothing like batteries. But plotted the same way, the cost picture has a familiar shape.

The cultivated points are not a clean series. Early figures are research costs for tiny batches, and the lowest, a company-reported cost for raw cell mass, hasn't been independently audited. The retail point is a product that is 40% cultivated cells. The log scale means each gridline is ten times the one below, so the line has fallen about five orders of magnitude.

Two things stand out. Cultivated costs are falling from far above, and conventional meat is rising from below. US beef prices are up 57% since 2020 as drought shrank the national cattle herd to its smallest in 75 years. Like gas plants in the energy chart, the incumbent is getting more expensive at the same time the challenger is getting cheaper.

What cheap batteries unlocked

Falling prices didn't create one market. They opened a sequence of them, each larger and more price-sensitive than the last.

Grid storage is now the fastest-growing part of the market. In the US, 57.6 GWh were installed in 2025, four times the total of three years earlier. Texas is on track to overtake California as the largest US storage market in 2026. Battery makers that built factories for EVs are retooling lines for grid storage: Korean battery maker LG Energy Solution is converting a Michigan EV battery plant to make 30 GWh of storage a year.

The key insight

This is the classic pattern of disruptive innovation: a new technology starts in markets the incumbent can't serve, improves on the revenue they bring in, then moves into the mainstream. Laptop buyers funded the factories. Those factories' cells powered the first Tesla. EV volume pushed costs low enough for the grid. No stage had to wait for the final price.

How batteries pulled it off, and what it means for lab-grown meat

Technology alone didn't bend the curve. Five forces stacked on top of each other. For each, here's how it worked for batteries, where else it has happened, and what it would take for cultivated meat.

1. A learning curve that rewards volume

How batteries did it

Every doubling of cumulative lithium-ion production has cut unit costs by roughly a fifth. That relationship, known as a learning curve or Wright's law, held for three decades because battery making is a repeatable factory process: bigger plants, better yields, cheaper materials, and better chemistry all compound with volume.

Also seen in: solar panels (roughly 20% cheaper per doubling for four decades), semiconductors, and industrial fermentation, where amino acids like lysine are now made at million-tonne scale for about a dollar a kilogram.

What it means for cultivated meatShowing signs

Cultivated meat costs have collapsed in a decade, mostly from engineering rather than volume: cheaper feed, cells that need fewer additives, and tanks designed for food instead of medicine. That is encouraging, because it means the curve hasn't yet been driven by scale at all.

The open question is whether animal cells behave like batteries or like pharmaceuticals. Biopharma did ride a learning curve: antibody production costs fell from thousands of dollars per gram to tens. But they have plateaued around $50–100 per gram, still thousands of times too expensive for food. The industries that reached commodity prices in bioreactors use microbes, which grow far faster and more robustly than animal cells.

2. Beachhead markets that pay for the next step

How batteries did it

Batteries never had to be cheap enough for the grid on day one. They started where buyers paid a premium for something new. Laptops and phones needed portable power, and that revenue built the factories.

China added a second kind of beachhead: the state as buyer. Its 2009 program put electric buses and taxis into government fleets before consumers were ready, giving manufacturers like BYD, now one of the world's largest EV makers, guaranteed early demand.

Also seen in: solar, whose first customers were satellites, then oil platforms, telecom relays and calculators; and lab-grown diamonds, which spent decades as industrial abrasives before becoming jewelry.

What it means for cultivated meatPartial

This is cultivated meat's biggest structural gap. Batteries offered something new (portability). Cultivated meat mostly offers the same thing at a higher price. Its strongest early markets are ones where conventional supply is scarce or banned (foie gras, bluefin tuna), where buyers care less about naturalness (pet food), or where the product is invisible (cultivated fat blended into other foods).

The battery precedent suggests two shortcuts: sell profitably at each step down the cost curve rather than waiting for parity, and use institutional buyers such as militaries or public food programs as the equivalent of China's bus fleets.

3. Demand that pulls, not just pushes

How batteries did it

Cheap solar created the market for storage. Once panels flooded the grid at midday, power became nearly worthless at noon and expensive at 7 p.m., and batteries could profit from moving it. Data centers then added fast-growing demand that gas turbines, now on multi-year backlogs, could not meet quickly. Energy shocks, from Russia's invasion of Ukraine to the 2026 conflict in the Middle East, added a security premium on top.

Also seen in: COVID vaccines, where urgency plus guaranteed buyers compressed a decade of scale-up into a year; and the 1970s oil shocks, which produced fuel-economy standards that roughly doubled car efficiency over the following decades.

What it means for cultivated meatBuilding

Global meat demand keeps rising (the UN expects 13% more by 2032), and protein has become a priority for GLP-1 users and fitness-minded consumers. But demand for meat is not the same as demand for cultivated meat. The pull here is indirect: it comes from the incumbent getting more expensive and less reliable, which we look at in detail below.

4. A sponsor with patience and a strategic motive

How batteries did it

China treated EVs and batteries as a national priority: to cut dependence on imported oil, leapfrog foreign automakers it could never beat on combustion engines, and create industrial jobs. CSIS, a Washington think tank, estimates government support of at least $230 billion between 2009 and 2023, nearly a fifth of the value of all EVs sold over that period. Around 200 EV makers competed, and most lost. The survivors became world leaders.

Western battery startups funded mainly by venture capital often failed in the same period: A123 Systems went bankrupt in 2012 and Sweden's Northvolt in 2024.

Also seen in: Taiwan's TSMC, spun out of a state research institute in 1987; France's state-led nuclear buildout.

What it means for cultivated meatMostly missing

All cultivated meat investment through 2024 totaled about $3 billion, roughly what China spent supporting EVs in a single slow year. Venture funding peaked near $1 billion in 2021 and fell to $74 million in 2025. Venture capital expects returns in 7–10 years; batteries took 30.

Sponsors are starting to appear outside the US. China's 2026–30 plan folds new proteins into its food-security strategy, and Singapore, the Netherlands and Gulf states have funded the sector. Food security for import-dependent countries is the closest analogue to China's oil-security motive for EVs.

5. Rules that reward the new technology

How batteries did it

Market design mattered as much as subsidies. Texas runs an energy-only power market that pays generously during shortages, so batteries earn money on price spikes, and its fast interconnection process lets them get built quickly. Texas has installed more solar and storage than any other state over the past three years, with no mandate. Federal storage tax credits also survived the 2025 cuts to wind and solar credits. China, meanwhile, required many new solar and wind farms to add batteries.

Also seen in: Germany's feed-in tariffs, which guaranteed prices for solar power; and US shale, where private mineral rights let wildcatters move fast once federal research had proven the technique.

What it means for cultivated meatHostile in places

Approval pathways now exist in Singapore, the US, Australia and elsewhere, which took years to build. But seven US states, including Texas, ban sales outright, the EU has banned meat-related names for alternatives, and Italy banned cultivated meat altogether. Rules can block a technology for a long time, as margarine laws did for decades, but history suggests they rarely hold forever once the price gap gets large.

The case it scales

Costs have already beaten the limits skeptics set in 2021, by wide margins on feed and cell density. Purpose-built food-grade tanks cost a fraction of pharma equipment. Tonne-scale runs are now happening, and leading companies say they are near profitability on each unit sold.

None of this required a breakthrough in science, only engineering. That is the pattern batteries followed.

The case it doesn't

Animal cells grow slowly, damage easily and are vulnerable to contamination, which makes very large, very clean tanks expensive. The cheapest inputs only get cheap once volume exists. The best cost figures are unaudited claims for raw cell mass, not finished meat.

Industries that reached commodity prices in bioreactors, like amino acids, use microbes, not animal cells.

Tailwinds and headwinds

Three forces acting on cultivated meat right now that the five forces above don't fully capture, and what the battery story suggests about each.

Tailwind

Scarcity that lasts, not just spikes

The headline shocks get attention. New World screwworm, a flesh-eating parasite, was confirmed in a Texas calf in June 2026, and the US had already closed live cattle imports from Mexico. The bigger force is slower: drought has shrunk the US cattle herd to its smallest in 75 years, and rebuilding takes years because ranchers must hold back heifers to breed. US beef prices are up 57% since 2020.

The battery lesson: Acute shocks, like oil spikes in the 1970s, in 2022 and again in 2026, create bursts of interest that fade when prices fall. What made batteries inevitable was sustained, structural demand from electrification and data centers, plus policies that outlasted the spikes. The 1970s oil shocks mattered because they produced fuel-economy rules that stayed. For meat, a lasting supply problem matters more than any single outbreak.

Tailwind

Protein demand is rising, and getting more specific

Global meat consumption is projected to grow 13% by 2032. Demand is also becoming more particular: GLP-1 users need more protein in smaller portions, and fitness-minded buyers want high protein with less fat. Blending cultivated cells or fat with plant or fungal protein makes it possible to tune protein, fat and fiber in ways a conventional cut cannot.

The battery lesson: Data centers didn't just add demand, they added a specific kind of demand (fast to build, available on short notice) that incumbent gas plants couldn't supply quickly. Batteries won share by solving the specific need first.

Headwind

The chicken-and-egg input problem

Engineer David Humbird, whose skeptical 2021 analysis is still the most cited critique of the industry, makes a point that still stands: the price of amino acids, growth factors and food-grade equipment depends on market volume, so none of it gets cheap until people are already eating cultivated meat at scale. Someone has to build supply before demand exists.

The battery lesson: China built lithium refining and cathode capacity ahead of demand, then sold into the market it created. Whoever builds the input supply chain for cultivated meat will likely capture the same leverage.

Consumer sentiment: the force batteries never faced

Nobody asks where their electrons come from. Meat is bought by billions of people, and it carries culture, place and identity.

This may be the single biggest difference between the two stories. Many people today would never eat "lab-grown" meat, and for many of them the objection isn't price or taste. It's principle. Fewer than half of consumers have even heard of cultivated meat, and only a small share say they would try it.

The resistance is also political. Texas shows the split clearly. The same state leads the country in batteries and bans cultivated meat. Batteries paid landowners lease income, added to county tax bases and changed nothing about anyone's daily life. Cultivated meat threatens an industry that defines rural identity and asks people to change something intimate about how they eat. Layer on the wider current against "ultra-processed" food and toward "natural" eating, and cultivated meat is swimming against several cultural tides at once.

Why this matters for the curve

For batteries, crossing price parity was enough, because the buyers were rational and invisible. For meat, price parity is necessary but probably not sufficient. Something else has to shift too.

What has overridden food identity before

Margarine

Price won

Dairy states banned or restricted it for decades (Wisconsin until 1967). It won on price anyway.

Food irradiation

Stigma won

FDA-approved for decades, yet consumer fear kept adoption tiny. Approval is not adoption.

Pasteurization

Regulation flipped it

Resisted as unnatural, then mandated after milk-borne disease outbreaks. Purity rules can become a tailwind.

GMOs

Split by market

Largely rejected in Europe; quietly accepted as everyday ingredients in the US.

IVF

Normalized

From 'test-tube babies' to routine medicine within a generation.

Lab-grown diamonds

Price won

A heavily marketed 'natural' incumbent lost younger buyers on value.

Putting it together: a projected curve

An illustrative sketch, not a forecast. Which branch we end up on depends on whether costs keep falling at commercial scale, whether a patient sponsor emerges, and whether products can reach shoppers without forcing an identity choice.

The bull case follows solar: costs keep falling at scale, a state sponsor commits, and processed cultivated meat reaches parity in the 2030s. The base case follows the invisible-ingredient path: cultivated cells and fat become a valuable minority ingredient in hybrid products, with fungal and plant proteins carrying most of the volume. The bear case follows cellulosic ethanol, where costs stall at near-pharmaceutical levels and the product stays a luxury niche no matter how much money goes in.

Hockey sticks have long handles

Years from first commercial sale to mass-market takeoff. Cultivated meat is in year six.

Seen this way, the slow start isn't evidence of failure. Lithium-ion took nearly three decades to reach its grid inflection and solar about four. Lab-grown diamonds did it in about fifteen. Mycoprotein, sold as Quorn since 1985, is a reminder that some technologies never get their takeoff. Cultivated meat's first decade looks like the early stretch of every curve that eventually bent, and it also looks like the early stretch of the ones that didn't. The signposts below are how to tell the difference.

What to watch

If most of these happen, the curve looks like solar. If costs stall at commercial scale, it looks like cellulosic ethanol.

  • Independently audited cost per kg from a 10,000-liter-plus production line
  • Feed prices confirmed by a third party, not just company or investor reports
  • Months of continuous production without contamination
  • China's first national approval of cultivated meat
  • A rising cultivated share inside hybrid products
  • A major meat company signing a long-term contract to buy cultivated cells
  • Court rulings on the Texas and Florida bans
The bottom line

Batteries looked hopeless right up until they didn't. The turn came not from a single breakthrough but from a learning curve, a chain of markets that paid for each step, a patient sponsor and rules that rewarded the new technology, all lining up at once.

Cultivated meat has the first of those and pieces of the rest. If a state sponsor commits and hybrid products find a profitable foothold, the next decade could look a lot like batteries' last one. If not, it stays a luxury. Either way, the people saying "never" are making the same bet the battery skeptics lost.
